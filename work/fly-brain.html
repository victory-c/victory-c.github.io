fly-brain

A fruit fly's whole central nervous system — 166,700 neurons from the male CNS connectome — run as a spiking network in PyTorch and put in charge of a road bike.

The idea

The male fruit-fly CNS connectome (Janelia FlyEM / Google, 2026) maps every neuron and chemical synapse in the animal — 166,700 neurons and about 124M synapses. Shiu et al. (Nature 2024) showed a simple leaky integrate-and-fire model on a fly connectome reproduces real feeding behaviour. I wanted to know what that brain does when you hand it a bicycle.

The simulator

A PyTorch reimplementation of Shiu's whole-brain model, run on the male CNS:

  • Spike for spike. A test builds the same random network in Shiu's Brian2 code and in this engine and requires identical spikes at identical steps. Getting there meant matching two Brian2 details most people never notice (Poisson-driven neurons have no refractory period; input during a refractory period is dropped, not held).
  • Many flies per GPU. A batched, event-driven engine plus a static-shape CUDA Graph version: 2.1 s per simulated second for one fly instead of 7.5 s.
  • Calibrated, not tuned. The synaptic weight is rescaled for the male CNS's denser wiring by rerunning Shiu's own sugar → MN9 feeding experiment — and the README says plainly where the male CNS can't match the paper.

The bike

Bike state is turned into firing of identified balance, wind, optic-flow and leg sensory neurons; 42 descending and motor neuron types are decoded into steering torque. The connectome is never changed — only an 86-number decoder is learned, by evolutionary search on the OCF Slurm cluster.

A blank decoder learns to ride in 37 generations of 48 riders: in the last generation 35 of 48 ride the full 15 s in side gusts and none falls. The honest caveat is on the page too — at that speed the bike is largely self-stable, so the harder runs hold it at 4.0–4.5 m/s, where riders without steering fall within seconds and the learned decoder keeps them up for 9.6–13.7 s on average.

What I learned

  • Reproducing a paper exactly is a feature, not a chore — the spike-for-spike test is what makes every later result worth reading.
  • Every Slurm job is logged with its exact command line, so any number on the site traces back to a run.
  • The caveats belong on the demo page, not buried in a footnote.
